Los Alamos • 1946
A Card Game
Sparks an Idea
A scientist named Stanislaw Ulam was resting after an illness, playing solitaire. He asked a simple question: what are the chances this game can be won? The math to work it out was far too hard. So he had a simpler idea. Play many random games, and just count how many you win.

Monaco • The Name
Why “Monte Carlo”?
My uncle would borrow money from relatives because he ‘just had to go to Monte Carlo’ to gamble.
Stanislaw Ulam (as told to Nicholas Metropolis, 1947)
The project was classified. They needed a code name. Ulam's gambling uncle, and Monaco's legendary Belle Époque casino, gave it one. The name captured the soul of the method: chance as a tool for truth.
ENIAC • 1948
The First Real
Monte Carlo Runs
John von Neumann turned Ulam's idea into the first Monte Carlo program. In 1948 his team ran it on ENIAC, one of the first electronic computers. Their target: the physics inside a nuclear weapon.
Los Alamos • Inside the Core
The Real Problem: Grow or Fizzle?
atom splits, releases more neutrons
neutron escapes the core
One neutron splits an atom into two neutrons, then four, then more. The paths are random and endless, with no formula to solve them. So they traced thousands of random neutrons and simply counted: does the reaction grow into a bomb, or fizzle out?
Origins & History
From Solitaire to Spreadsheets
1777: Buffon's Needle
Comte de Buffon drops needles on parallel lines to estimate π. The first recorded use of randomness to solve a deterministic math problem.
1946: Ulam's Solitaire Epiphany
Stanislaw Ulam, recovering from illness at Los Alamos, realizes playing thousands of random solitaire games could give the win probability. He immediately applies the idea to neutron diffusion in fissile material, the core physics of the H-bomb.
1947: Von Neumann & the Name
Ulam tells John von Neumann, who writes the first statistical neutron code in March 1947. Nicholas Metropolis dubs it the "Monte Carlo method" after Ulam's gambling uncle and the Monaco casino.
1948: ENIAC Runs It
Klara von Neumann, Metropolis, and team execute the first computerized MC on ENIAC, one of the first programs in the "stored program" paradigm. Enables the Teller-Ulam H-bomb design (1951).
1949: First Public Paper
Metropolis & Ulam publish "The Monte Carlo Method" in the Journal of the American Statistical Association, the first unclassified paper. The method goes public.
1980s+: Business Adoption
Spreadsheets and tools like @RISK, Crystal Ball, and Excel bring MC to capital budgeting, option pricing, project risk, the standard today in engineering economy.
The Method
How Monte Carlo Works
Instead of solving a problem analytically with fixed inputs, simulate it thousands of times with randomly sampled inputs and observe the distribution of outcomes.
1

Define Uncertainty

Identify which inputs are uncertain and assign probability distributions (Normal, Triangular, Uniform)

2

Random Sampling

Draw random values from each distribution using RNG (e.g., Box-Muller for Normal)

3

Compute Output

Run your model (NPV, AW, IRR) with the sampled inputs to get one outcome

4

Repeat & Analyze

Do this 1,000-10,000+ times. Build histograms, find P(loss), percentiles, CI

Error shrinks as 1/√n  →  10× more iterations = 3.16× more precise
Classic Example
Estimating π with Random Darts
Throw random darts at a 1×1 square containing a quarter circle of radius 1. The fraction landing inside approximates π/4.
π ≈ 4 × (points inside circle) / (total points)

💡 Law of Large Numbers

As samples grow, the sample ratio converges to the true area ratio. Try +1 first, then +1000. Watch the estimate stabilize.

The Core Lesson
Deterministic vs Stochastic

❌ Deterministic Model

  • Single "best guess" for each input
  • One output: NPV = $41,913
  • Decision: Accept, looks profitable
  • No visibility into risk or downside
  • False confidence in a single number

✅ Monte Carlo Model

  • Distributions capture real uncertainty
  • Full range: -$150K to +$250K
  • 43% chance of negative NPV revealed
  • P(Bonus) = 38%, bonus is unlikely
  • Decision made with eyes open
Uncertainty is not the enemy, ignoring it is.
