From the will
"The capital shall be invested by my executors in safe securities and shall constitute a fund, the interest on which shall be annually distributed in the form of prizes to those who, during the preceding year, shall have conferred the greatest benefit on mankind."
— Alfred Nobel, 27 November 1895
ref: Wikipedia — Nobel Foundation · Alfred Nobel
Pattern recognition

"The interest on which shall be annually distributed" is the textbook definition of a perpetual cash flow: a fixed annual payout A drawn from a fund earning interest i, never touching the principal.

The trick of the math

The fund size needed for an infinite stream is not infinite. It is finite, and surprisingly small: CC = A / i

A sanity check

5 prizes × ~310 k SEK each (the original prize size) ≈ 1.55 M SEK / yr. At i ≈ 5% safe-bond rates of 1895, that needs a fund of ~31 M SEK — exactly what Nobel left.

Definition
For a uniform perpetual cash flow A at MARR i:
$$\mathrm{CC} \;=\; \frac{A}{i}$$
i.e., the present worth of an infinite series of equal payments.

1
CC is just PW of an infinite annuity. The (P/A, i, n) factor converges to 1/i as n → ∞.
2
CC is extremely sensitive to i: halving the MARR doubles the fund you need. Pick i carefully.
3
For one-time costs that repeat every L years (e.g. replacing a roof every 30 yr), use A = cost × (A/F, i, L) first, then CC = A / i.

How much of forever do you actually pay for?

A perpetuity sounds expensive, but at typical MARRs the long tail is almost free. You buy most of "forever" in the first 30 to 50 years.
3 / 3 · the long view

Fraction of CC you have captured after n years

PW(n) / CC = 1 − (1 + i)−n
At MARR 5%, the first 50 years of a perpetual stream already captures 91% of its present worth. The remaining centuries are worth less than the first half-century. You buy most of "forever" in the first lifetime.
