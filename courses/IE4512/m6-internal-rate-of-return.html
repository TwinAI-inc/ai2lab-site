Pattern recognition

Given a present worth P, a future worth F, and a horizon n, the rate that makes the cash flow balance is the internal rate of return: the discount rate r at which NPV(r) = 0.

The trick of the math

For one outflow and one inflow, IRR has a closed form: r = (F / P)1/n − 1

A sanity check

$24 → $3 trillion across 398 years gives r ≈ 6.6%. The lesson: at long horizons, modest rates do epic work. The horizon dominates the rate.

Definition
For a single outflow P today and a single inflow F after n periods:
\[r \;=\; \left(\frac{F}{P}\right)^{1/n} - 1\]
the IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV equal to zero.

1
For one outflow and one inflow, IRR has a closed form. For arbitrary cash flows it does not, and we solve numerically.
2
IRR is brutal at short horizons, gentle at long ones. Double the horizon, halve the required rate roughly.

Compare IRR to MARR. If IRR > MARR, the project clears the hurdle. Otherwise, walk away.

How compounding beats the speed limit

At long horizons, a modest rate builds a fortune. At short horizons, even a stellar rate builds a salary. The horizon dominates the rate.

At 7%, $24 becomes more than $10 trillion in 400 years — three times Manhattan's land value today. The Lenape's best alternative could have beaten the deal — but only because compounding had four centuries to work. The horizon dominates the rate.
