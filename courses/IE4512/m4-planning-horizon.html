How long should we look ahead?

When competing options have different useful lives, we can't just compare them as-is. The analyst picks a planning horizon — and that choice can flip the verdict. Click each card to see the six standard methods, then run them all on the same example below.

Method 1

Least Common Multiple

Use the LCM of all useful lives as the horizon. Each option replicates its full life cycle (same P, AOC, SV) until the horizon is reached — every option ends at exactly year LCM with no leftover.

Use whenidentical replacements are plausible — stable technology, no obsolescence, repetitive purchase decisions.
HorizonT = LCM(nA, nB, nC, …)
Method 02
②
Longest Life
match the longest-lived option
tap to flip
Method 2

Longest Life

Set the horizon equal to the longest useful life. Shorter-lived options must replicate (and possibly truncate) to fill the horizon; the longest-lived option runs its full cycle once.

Use whenthe longest-lived option naturally caps the decision — e.g., a building lease determines how long the equipment matters.
HorizonT = max(nA, nB, nC, …)

③
Shortest Life

Method 3

Shortest Life

Use the shortest useful life. Longer-lived options must be sold prematurely at the horizon; we estimate a partial-cycle salvage value (linear book-value approximation) for the truncation.

Use whenuncertainty or technology change makes long-term commitments risky — short horizon is conservative.
HorizonT = min(nA, nB, nC, …)

Method 4

Standard Horizon

Use a firm-wide policy horizon (often 5 or 10 years). Every option replicates and truncates as needed to fit. Most common in practice because it makes capital projects comparable across the company.

Use whenyour firm has a consistent capital budgeting horizon — keeps the analysis apples-to-apples across all projects.
HorizonT = company standard (e.g., 10 yr)

Method 5

Organizational Need

Set the horizon to the actual need: contract length, product lifecycle, expected facility tenure. The most honest method when the decision is project-specific rather than asset-life-driven.

Use whenyou can name the real planning need — e.g., "we need this equipment for 7 years to finish the contract".
HorizonT = project-specific (user picks)

Method 6

Infinitely Long

Assume perpetual repetition. Compare via Annual Worth (AW) directly — smaller AW wins. Equivalent to comparing Capitalized Cost CC = AW / i (the PW of a perpetual cash flow).

Use whenthe service is essential indefinitely — utilities, water mains, infrastructure where "we'll always need this".
Decisionmin AW   or   CCk = AWk / i
