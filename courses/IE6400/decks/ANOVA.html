THE PROBLEM

Why ANOVA?

THE SCENARIO

You have 3 or more groups and want to know if their means differ significantly.

For example: Do three machines produce different output rates? Do four teaching methods yield different test scores?

WHY NOT MULTIPLE T-TESTS?

With 3 groups, you need 3 pairwise t-tests. Each at $\alpha = 0.05$ inflates your overall error rate:

$$\alpha_{\text{overall}} = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^k$$

For $k=3$ comparisons:

$$\alpha_{\text{overall}} = 1 - (0.95)^3 \approx 0.143$$
⚠
TYPE I ERROR INFLATION

Instead of a 5% chance of a false positive, you now have a 14.3% chance. With 10 groups and 45 comparisons, it jumps to ~90%! ANOVA solves this with a single, unified test.

A DEEPER LOOK

ANOVA vs Multiple t-tests

PAIRWISE t-TESTS (NAIVE)

With $k$ groups, you need $\binom{k}{2}$ tests:

  • • 3 groups → 3 tests
  • • 5 groups → 10 tests
  • • 10 groups → 45 tests
$\alpha_{\text{overall}} = 1 - (1-\alpha)^m$

Fails to control family-wise error rate.

ANOVA (ONE TEST)

A single omnibus F-test asks: do any of the means differ?

  • • One hypothesis, one $\alpha$
  • • Error rate stays at $0.05$
  • • Scales to any $k$
$F = \dfrac{MS_B}{MS_W}$

Then use post-hoc to locate the differences.

💡

Special case: When $k=2$ (two groups), ANOVA and the independent-samples t-test are algebraically identical — $F = t^2$ and both give the same p-value.

CORE CONCEPT

The ANOVA Idea

Total Variation = Between Group Variation + Within Group Variation

$$SS_T = SS_B + SS_W$$
📈
SST (TOTAL)

How much do all observations vary from the grand mean?

$$SS_T = \sum_{i}\sum_{j}(x_{ij} - \bar{x}_{\cdot\cdot})^2$$
↔
SSB (BETWEEN)

How much do group means vary from the grand mean?

$$SS_B = \sum_{i} n_i(\bar{x}_{i\cdot} - \bar{x}_{\cdot\cdot})^2$$
⇵
SSW (WITHIN)

How much do observations vary within each group?

$$SS_W = \sum_{i}\sum_{j}(x_{ij} - \bar{x}_{i\cdot})^2$$
💡

Key insight: If the between group spread is much larger than the within group spread, the groups are likely different.

SEEING THE PARTITION

Analogy — Salaries Across Departments

SSB — BETWEEN DEPARTMENTS

How far does each department's average salary sit from the company-wide average?

SSW — WITHIN EACH DEPARTMENT

How much do individual salaries vary from their own department's mean? (Junior vs senior, bonuses, etc.)

THE QUESTION ANOVA ANSWERS

Is the department effect (between) big enough relative to the individual noise (within) to conclude departments actually pay differently?

HOW TO RUN AN ANOVA

The 5-Step Procedure

1
HYPOTHESES

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \ldots = \mu_k$
$H_1$: at least one differs

2
SUM OF SQUARES

Compute $SS_B$, $SS_W$, $SS_T$ from group means and the grand mean.

3
F-STATISTIC

$F = \dfrac{MS_B}{MS_W}$
with $df_1 = k{-}1$, $df_2 = N{-}k$

4
CRITICAL / P

Look up $F_{\alpha,\;df_1,\;df_2}$ in the F-table, or compute p-value.

5
DECISION

If $F > F_{\text{crit}}$ (or $p < \alpha$), reject $H_0$. Then run post-hoc.

🔎

Before Step 1: always check assumptions (independence, normality, equal variance). If any fail, switch to Welch's ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis.

THE TEST STATISTIC

The F-Statistic

FORMULA
$$F = \frac{MS_B}{MS_W} = \frac{SS_B \;/\; (k-1)}{SS_W \;/\; (N-k)}$$
$k$ = number of groups
$N$ = total observations
$MS_B$ = mean square between
$MS_W$ = mean square within
📚

Under $H_0$, $F$ follows the F-distribution with $(k{-}1,\; N{-}k)$ degrees of freedom.

KEY PROPERTIES
  • ✓ $F \geq 0$ always
  • ✓ Right skewed distribution
  • ✓ Large $F$ = evidence of difference
  • ✓ $F \approx 1$ under $H_0$
DECISION RULE

Reject $H_0$ if $F > F_{\alpha,\;k-1,\;N-k}$

Or equivalently: reject if p-value $< \alpha$

VISUALIZING F

The F-Distribution & the P-value

P-VALUE

The area under the F-curve to the right of the observed $F$. Small p-value → observed $F$ is far out in the tail.

REJECTION REGION

Set by $\alpha$: the critical value $F_\alpha$ cuts off the top $\alpha$ of the distribution.

BEFORE YOU TEST

ANOVA Assumptions

Independence

Observations within and across groups are independent of each other.

If violated: Use repeated measures ANOVA or mixed effects models.

Normality

Each group's data is approximately normally distributed.

If violated: Use Kruskal-Wallis test (non parametric alternative).

Equal Variance

All groups have similar variance $\sigma^2$ (homoscedasticity).

If violated: Use Welch's ANOVA or transform data.

🔎

Pro tip: ANOVA is robust to mild violations of normality (especially with balanced group sizes). Check with Levene's test for equal variance and Shapiro-Wilk for normality.

FAMILY OF ANOVAs

Types of ANOVA

ONE-WAY
One Factor, $k$ Levels

One categorical predictor, one numeric outcome. Compare means across $k$ groups.

EXAMPLE

3 machines → output

df: $(k{-}1,\; N{-}k)$
TWO-WAY
Two Factors + Interaction

Two categorical predictors. Tests main effects of each, plus their interaction.

EXAMPLE

Machine × shift → output

Tests: factor A, factor B, A×B
REPEATED MEASURES
Same Subjects, Over Time

Each subject measured under multiple conditions. Accounts for within-subject correlation.

EXAMPLE

Patient BP at 0, 4, 8 weeks

Use: same-subject designs, growth curves
🔑

Also worth knowing: MANOVA (multiple outcome variables), ANCOVA (ANOVA with a continuous covariate), mixed effects ANOVA (fixed + random factors). All share the same variance partition logic.

AFTER ANOVA

Post-Hoc: Tukey's HSD

THE PROBLEM

ANOVA tells you "at least one mean is different" but does not tell you which ones differ.

Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) compares all pairs while controlling the family-wise error rate.

FORMULA
$$HSD = q_{\alpha,\;k,\;N-k} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{MS_W}{n}}$$

$q$ = Studentized Range statistic, $n$ = observations per group

Rule: If $|\bar{x}_i - \bar{x}_j| > HSD$, then groups $i$ and $j$ differ significantly.

APPLYING TO OUR EXAMPLE

With $q_{0.05,\;3,\;12} \approx 3.77$, $MS_W = 2.3$, $n = 5$:

$$HSD = 3.77 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2.3}{5}} = 3.77 \times 0.678 = 2.56$$
PAIRWISE COMPARISONS
✗ $|A - B| = |24.2 - 30.0| = 5.8 > 2.56$ → Significant
✓ $|A - C| = |24.2 - 25.0| = 0.8 < 2.56$ → Not significant
✗ $|B - C| = |30.0 - 25.0| = 5.0 > 2.56$ → Significant

Machine B is significantly faster than both A and C.

HOW BIG IS THE EFFECT?

Effect Size & Interpretation

ETA SQUARED ($\eta^2$)
$$\eta^2 = \frac{SS_B}{SS_T}$$

The proportion of total variance explained by the grouping factor. Our example: $\eta^2 = 98.8/126.4 = 0.78$ → 78% of variation is explained by the machine.

RULES OF THUMB
  • • $\eta^2 \approx 0.01$ → small effect
  • • $\eta^2 \approx 0.06$ → medium effect
  • • $\eta^2 \geq 0.14$ → large effect
WHAT "REJECT H0" ACTUALLY MEANS

At least one group mean differs from the others — not that all means differ. Post-hoc tests locate the differences.

COMMON MISREADS
  • • "$p < 0.05$" means the effect is important — no, it means unlikely under $H_0$
  • • A non-significant F means "no difference" — no, it means insufficient evidence

Report both: the F-statistic and $\eta^2$. Statistical significance tells you whether, effect size tells you how much.

WATCH OUT

Common Pitfalls

⚠
RUNNING MULTIPLE t-TESTS

Skipping ANOVA and running all pairwise t-tests inflates the family-wise error rate. Always start with the omnibus F.

⚠
IGNORING ASSUMPTIONS

Severely unequal variances or heavy skew can invalidate the F-test. Check Levene's / Shapiro-Wilk before trusting the p-value.

⚠
UNEQUAL SAMPLE SIZES

Highly unbalanced designs amplify the effect of heteroscedasticity. Prefer balanced groups or switch to Welch's ANOVA.

⚠
STOPPING AT THE F-TEST

A significant F alone says nothing about which groups differ. Always follow with Tukey's HSD (or Bonferroni / Scheffe).

⚠
INTERPRETING MAIN EFFECTS UNDER INTERACTION

In two-way ANOVA, if the A×B interaction is significant, main effects can be misleading — interpret simple effects instead.

⚠
CONFUSING SIGNIFICANCE WITH SIZE

With very large $n$, tiny mean differences can produce $p < 0.05$. Always report $\eta^2$ alongside the F to show how much the factor matters.
